By the end of this lab, you will be able to:

  • Filler Cell Insertion: Insert standard cell filler cells in decreasing size order to eliminate gaps between standard cells and maintain N-well continuity across the row.
  • Metal Fill / Dummy Fill: Run timing-aware metal fill to insert floating metal shapes on all interconnect layers, meeting CMP density requirements without degrading critical path timing.

💡 Chip Finish is the last ICC2 stage before the design leaves the physical implementation team. Every error caught here prevents a costly silicon re-spin.

📖 Theory and Background

What is Chip Finish?

Chip Finish is the final stage of physical implementation in the ASIC design flow. It takes the post-route optimized block — where all signal nets are routed and timing is closed — and prepares it for tape-out. No logic optimization or placement changes happen here; instead, the focus is on:

  • Filling physical gaps in standard cell rows (filler insertion)
  • Inserting dummy metal to meet foundry density rules (metal fill)
  • Running final design verification (DRC and LVS)
  • Exporting the design database for downstream sign-off tools (write_verilog, write_gds, write_def)

After a successful chip finish run, the GDSII file is submitted to the foundry for fabrication, the gate-level Verilog netlist (LEC version) is handed to the equivalence checking team, and the DEF is delivered for back-annotation into the STA sign-off flow.

📄 Filler Cell Insertion

Standard cells are placed in rows that share a common power rail at a fixed pitch. When the placer cannot fit logic cells into every slot in a row, gaps appear. These gaps cause two physical problems:

  • N-well discontinuity: PMOS transistors in standard cells require a continuous N-well region. A gap in the cell row breaks this well, causing substrate leakage and potential latch-up.
  • Power rail gap: The VDD and VSS rails running through the standard cell row are implemented as metal shapes inside each cell. A physical gap leaves an open in the power distribution network.

Filler cells (also called decap cells or gap-fill cells) are physical-only cells that contain no logic. They have the same height as standard cells, carry VDD and VSS pins, and extend the N-well continuously across each gap. ICC2 inserts them using create_stdcell_fillers.

Insertion order matters: Filler cells must be inserted in decreasing size order (FILL32 before FILL16 before FILL8 … before FILL1). ICC2 fills the largest available gaps with the largest filler cell first, then fills residual smaller gaps with progressively smaller cells. Inserting small fillers first would waste them on large gaps, potentially leaving gaps that the largest filler could have filled in one placement.

After filler insertion: Always run connect_pg_net immediately after create_stdcell_fillers. Filler cells expose VDD and VSS pins that must be connected to the power grid. Without this step, LVS will report open power pins on every filler cell — hundreds of thousands of opens for a large design.

📄 Metal Fill / Dummy Fill

Chemical Mechanical Polishing (CMP) is the wafer planarization process used between each metal layer deposition. CMP grinds the wafer surface flat using a chemical slurry and a polishing pad. The rate at which material is removed depends on local metal density — areas with dense metal polish slowly while areas with sparse metal polish quickly. This causes:

  • Dishing: Sparse metal regions are over-polished, causing the metal surface to bowl inward. Dished metal lines have higher resistance.
  • Erosion: Dense dielectric regions are over-polished next to dense metal, removing interlayer dielectric and reducing isolation between adjacent metal layers.

Foundries specify a minimum and maximum metal density rule per layer per window size. If a region of a metal layer is below the minimum density, the foundry requires dummy (floating) metal shapes to be added. These shapes are electrically floating — they carry no signal — but their physical presence stabilizes the CMP rate.

ICC2 inserts sign-off quality metal fill with signoff_create_metal_fill -track_fill generic. The -track_fill generic option aligns fill shapes to the routing track grid so they respect DRC rules from the technology file. This is a sign-off level command — more rigorous than the in-flow create_fill — and is the correct choice at the chip finish stage when preparing for foundry tape-out.

⚠️ Metal fill and routing DRC: Run route_detail -incremental true after filler insertion (before metal fill) to fix any DRC violations the fillers introduced. After signoff_create_metal_fill, re-run check_routes to confirm the metal fill shapes do not introduce new spacing violations.

✔️ Final DRC and LVS

Two final verification steps are required before any data can leave the implementation environment:

  • check_routes (DRC): Verifies all routed wires against the design rule deck — minimum metal spacing, minimum width, via enclosure, antenna ratio, and connectivity (no opens, no shorts). A clean check_routes report shows zero DRC errors and zero antenna violations.
  • check_lvs (LVS): Layout Versus Schematic verification. ICC2 compares the connectivity of the physical database (every net, pin, and device) against the logical netlist. It reports shorts (two nets shorted together in layout but not in schematic) and opens (a net connected in schematic but broken in layout). A clean LVS shows zero shorts and zero opens.
⚠️ check_routes vs. foundry DRC: check_routes is ICC2's internal router-aware DRC checker and uses a simplified rule set. It is mandatory to run but does not replace a full foundry DRC run (Calibre or ICV) on the final GDSII — that check happens after tape-out and uses the complete foundry PDK rule deck.

📄 Netlist, GDSII, and DEF Export

After verification passes, four output files are written:

  • write_verilog (LEC netlist): Writes a gate-level Verilog netlist for use by the LEC tool. Physical-only cells — filler cells, end-cap cells, well-tap cells, power/ground objects — must be excluded using -exclude. These cells have no logical equivalents in the reference netlist; including them causes false LEC failures. The -hierarchy all option preserves module boundaries for faster LEC runtime.
  • write_verilog (LVS netlist): A second Verilog write with a different -exclude set — only empty_modules are excluded so that all physical cells (fillers, end-caps, tap cells) are present. The LVS tool needs all physical instances to perform connectivity extraction against the layout.
  • write_gds (Tape-out database): Exports the complete physical layout as a GDSII stream file. The foundry uses this file to generate photomasks. Key options: -merge_files streams in standard cell GDS from PDK (LVT/SVT/HVT), -units 2000 sets TSMC 28nm DBU precision (2000 units/µm), and -layer_map maps ICC2 layer numbers to foundry layer/purpose pairs.
  • write_def (Back-annotation): Exports the full physical database in DEF format for back-annotation into PrimeTime or Tempus for sign-off STA. Uses -version 5.8 and -units 1000 (DEF precision is independent of GDS DBU).

📝 Knowledge Assessment Quiz

Test your understanding of the chip finish concepts covered above. Click on your answer.

1. Why must filler cells be inserted to fill gaps between standard cells?

  • Filler cells contain additional drive strength buffers that reduce output transition time on nearby signal nets.
  • The router requires every cell site to be occupied before it can complete via insertion on local metal layers.
  • Gaps in the standard cell row break the continuous N-well required for correct PMOS transistor operation and also create open segments in the VDD/VSS power rail running through the row.
  • Filler cells add decoupling capacitance that reduces IR drop on the power mesh above the standard cell rows.
Correct! Standard cell rows rely on a continuous N-well for PMOS transistors and continuous metal power rails. Physical gaps between logic cells break both. Filler cells are physical-only cells that bridge these gaps — they extend the N-well and carry VDD and VSS pins connected to the power grid — without adding any logic to the design.

2. What problem does CMP uniformity requirements solve, and why does the foundry care about metal density?

  • High metal density causes current crowding that increases electromigration risk on power straps.
  • Non-uniform metal density causes the CMP polishing rate to vary across the wafer, creating dishing in sparse regions and erosion in dense regions — both of which change the effective resistance and isolation of metal wires, leading to timing failures or shorts in the fabricated chip.
  • Metal density rules are required so that the fill shapes can serve as antenna discharge paths during plasma etching.
  • Uniform metal density reduces parasitic capacitance by ensuring equal spacing between all adjacent metal lines across the die.
Correct! CMP removes material at a rate proportional to local pattern density. Sparse metal areas are over-polished (dishing), increasing wire resistance, while dense dielectric areas erode, reducing inter-layer isolation. Foundry density rules specify minimum and maximum metal fill fractions per layer and per window to keep the CMP rate uniform across the die, ensuring predictable electrical characteristics in silicon.

3. What does check_lvs verify, and what does it mean if it reports a "short"?

  • check_lvs verifies wire spacing rules — a "short" means two parallel wires are spaced closer than the minimum DRC rule for that metal layer.
  • check_lvs verifies that all standard cell instances match their library views — a "short" means a cell has an incorrect drive strength relative to the timing constraint.
  • check_lvs compares the physical database connectivity against the logical netlist — a "short" means two electrically distinct nets in the schematic are physically connected in the layout (e.g., by an accidental metal shape overlap or missing cut), which would cause functional failure in silicon.
  • check_lvs verifies antenna ratios — a "short" means a metal wire is connected to too many gate oxide areas during plasma etching without a discharge path.
Correct! LVS (Layout Versus Schematic) extracts the connectivity of the physical database and compares it net-by-net against the logical netlist. A short in LVS means two logically independent nets are physically merged in the layout — for example, a misplaced via accidentally touches an adjacent signal wire. This is a functional failure: both nets are driven to the same voltage in silicon. LVS must show zero shorts and zero opens before any design can be taped out.

4. Why must filler cells, end-cap cells, and power/ground objects be excluded from the write_verilog output used for LEC?

  • These cells consume too much disk space — excluding them keeps the Verilog file manageable for the LEC tool to parse quickly.
  • The LEC tool cannot read hierarchical Verilog; excluding physical-only cells flattens the netlist to a single module.
  • Physical-only cells have no logical representation in the reference (synthesis) netlist. If they appear in the implementation netlist, the LEC tool cannot find a matching counterpart and reports false equivalence failures — making it impossible to determine whether real logic changes were introduced during physical implementation.
  • Excluding these cells is optional; it is done only to reduce the number of LEC mapping iterations required to achieve full equivalence.
Correct! LEC compares two netlists by establishing a one-to-one mapping between corresponding logic gates. Filler cells, end-cap cells, and well-tap cells are inserted purely for physical reasons — they have no logical function and do not exist in the synthesis reference netlist. Power/ground objects (pg_objects) are also implementation-only constructs. Including any of these in the write_verilog output causes the LEC tool to report unmapped points, which are indistinguishable from real logical changes introduced during ECO or buffer insertion. Excluding them produces a clean, logic-only netlist that the LEC tool can compare directly.
