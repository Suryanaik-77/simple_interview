🧠 Concept Overview

1) Wafer Preparation

Crystal Growth (Making the Silicon)

  • Raw Material: The process begins with quartz sand, which is abundant but contains impurities such as iron, aluminium, and other metals.
  • Initial Purification: The quartz sand is placed in an electric arc furnace with a carbon source and heated above 2,000°C.
  • A reduction reaction occurs, separating silicon from oxygen and producing metallurgical grade silicon (MG-Si)—about 98-99% pure, but still not enough for electronics.
  • Making Trichlorosilane: Metallurgical grade silicon is reacted with hydrogen chloride (HCl) gas at around 300°C in a fluidized bed reactor, forming trichlorosilane (SiHCl₃) gas and hydrogen. Impurities such as iron, aluminium, and boron react with chlorine and are removed.
  • Siemens Process: The purified trichlorosilane is decomposed in a chemical vapor deposition (CVD) reactor at about 1,100°C, depositing polycrystalline silicon onto heated silicon rods. This poly-silicon is now 99.9999% pure or better.

Czochralski Process

The Czochralski process is a widely used method for growing large, high-purity single crystals, especially silicon, for the electronics and semiconductor industries.

Melting High-Purity Silicon

  • Polycrystalline silicon (obtained from refining quartz sand through chemical processes) is loaded into a quartz crucible inside a pulling chamber.
  • The chamber is filled with an inert gas (usually argon) to avoid contamination from oxygen or moisture.
  • The silicon is heated above its melting point (about 1,425°C), forming a pool of molten silicon.

Image: A Silicon Crystal Growing Furnace (Czochralski Process)

Dipping the Seed Crystal

  • A small, precisely oriented seed crystal (a tiny piece of silicon with the desired crystal structure) is attached to a rod.
  • This rod is carefully lowered so the tip of the seed just touches the surface of the molten silicon

Pulling and Rotating

  • The seed crystal is slowly pulled upward from the melt, while rotating.
  • As the seed pulls away, silicon atoms in the melt begin to solidify onto the seed, forming a larger crystal with the same atomic arrangement (single crystal).
  • Both the pulling rate (how fast the rod is raised) and rotation speed are precisely controlled. These help maintain a uniform crystal diameter and quality.

Growing the Silicon ingot

  • The process continues, and a large cylindrical silicon ingot (boule) grows, hanging from the seed crystal.
  • This entire crystalline rod can be over a meter long and up to 300mm in diameter, depending on modern wafer specification.

Image: Silicon ingot & wafer

Wafer Slicing

Slicing the Silicon Ingot into Wafers

  • The main cutting method is diamond wire sawing:
    • A very fine wire coated with diamond particles is looped tightly and used to saw through the silicon ingot.
    • The wire moves at high speed while a slurry of water and abrasive particles cools and lubricates the cutting region.
    • This method provides a precise cut with minimal material lost during cutting.
  • Other methods include circular blade sawing (ID sawing) or multi-wire sawing, but diamond wire saws are preferred for precision

Thickness and Precision

  • Wafers are cut to a typical thickness of about 0.5–1 mm.
  • The slicing must be very precise to maintain uniform thickness and flat surfaces.
  • Fine control over slicing parameters such as wire tension, speed, and coolant flow ensures minimal damage.

Image: Ingot slicing

Wafer Handling Post-Slicing

  • Newly sliced wafers are very fragile and easily cracked or chipped.
  • To stabilize them, they are mounted on adhesive tapes (called wafer bonding films) for support.
  • Robotic automated systems help manage wafers through subsequent processes.

Edge Grinding and Shaping

  • After slicing, the wafer edges are often ground and rounded (chamfered) to remove any roughness or cracks caused by sawing.
  • This edge conditioning prevents chipping and damage during later handling.

This slicing process transforms a large silicon ingot into hundreds of thin wafers, each ready for further polishing, cleaning, and device fabrication steps.


Image: Wafer sizes

Typical wafer size

  • 6 inch (150nm), Thickness 675 um
  • 8 inch (200nm), Thickness 725 um
  • 12 inch (300nm), Thickness 775um

Mirror Finish

Using advanced polishing machines and fine abrasive, wafers' surfaces are polished to a mirror-like finish.

  • Sometimes single-side or double-side polishing is performed depending on the application.
  • The surface must be extremely flat and smooth, with only a few atomic layers of roughness.

RCA Cleaning Process

A series of chemical baths rigorously clean the surface:

  • Organic, metallic, and particle contaminations are removed.
  • Deionized water rinses clear away any leftover chemicals.
  • Ultrasonic cleaning or spin drying may also be used.
  • Employees wear special suits and cleanroom protection to avoid contaminating wafers during this step

Inspection

Quality Check: Final inspection involves using high-powered microscopes and analysis tools:

  • Checks for surface defects, flatness, thickness variance, and crystal orientation.
  • Only wafers meeting the required specifications proceed to the next microchip manufacturing stage.

Image: Wafer preparation process

2) MOSFET Preparation

1. Starting Substrate Preparation

  • Begin with a high-purity, lightly doped silicon wafer, typically p-type for NMOS fabrication.
  • The wafer is cleaned thoroughly to remove any contaminants.

Image: Substrate Preparation

2. Oxidation

  • The oxidation process forms a silicon dioxide layer that electrically insulates different parts of the device.
  • The silicon wafer is exposed to a high-temperature environment (between approximately 800°C and 1200°C) in a furnace or oxidation chamber.
  • An oxidizing agent, either dry oxygen (O2) or water vapor (H2O in the form of steam), is introduced.
  • Dry oxidation uses pure oxygen and produces a thinner, denser, and higher-quality oxide layer. It is commonly used for gate oxide formation in MOSFETs.

Dry oxidation: Si+O2→SiO2

  • Wet oxidation uses steam and grows oxide faster but results in a thicker and less dense oxide. It is typically used for thicker field oxide regions where speed is prioritized over quality.

Wet oxidation: Si+2H2O→SiO2+2H2


Image: Oxidation

3. Photoresist

  • A light-sensitive polymer called photoresist is applied to the wafer surface, which is usually covered by a layer of silicon dioxide (SiO2) or other film.
  • This produces a uniform, thin coating typically around 1 micrometre thick.
  • The wafer with the photoresist layer is then exposed to ultraviolet (UV) light through a patterned mask (photomask).
  • The photomask contains the desired circuit design pattern; areas exposed to light undergo a chemical change.
  • Depending on the type of photoresist:

Positive photoresist becomes soluble in the developer where exposed to light.

Negative photoresist hardens and becomes insoluble where exposed.


Image: Photo Resist

4. Photolithography

The photolithography process in MOSFET fabrication is a key step used to transfer a specific pattern onto the silicon wafer surface.

  • The wafer is aligned beneath a photomask containing the desired circuit pattern.
  • Ultraviolet (UV) light is shined through the photomask onto the photoresist.
  • The UV exposure causes a chemical change in the photoresist:
  • The remaining photoresist is baked again to harden it, improving resistance to subsequent processing like etching or ion implantation.
  • The patterned photoresist acts as a mask during etching, where exposed areas of the underlying oxide or silicon are removed.

Image: Photolithography

5. Etching

  • Etching in MOSFET fabrication is a fundamental process used to selectively remove material from the surface of the silicon wafer or other layers to create the desired patterns that define the device's structure.
  • It follows photolithography, where photoresist masks protect certain areas, and etched areas expose the underlying material for further processing.

There are two major types of etching used in MOSFET fabrication:

  1. Wet Etching:
    • Uses liquid chemical solutions called etchants to dissolve and remove the exposed material.
    • The wafer or substrate is either dipped into the bath of chemicals or the etchant is sprayed/spun onto the wafer.
    • Common wet etchants include hydrofluoric acid (HF) for silicon dioxide, nitric acid, potassium hydroxide (KOH), and phosphoric acid depending on the material to be etched.
    • Control over etch rate and uniformity depends on temperature, concentration of etchant, and duration of the process.
  2. Dry Etching:
    • Uses plasma (ionized gases) to remove materials by chemical reaction and physical bombardment.
    • This process is also known as plasma etching or reactive ion etching (RIE).
    • Types of dry etching include plasma etching, reactive ion etching (RIE), deep reactive ion etching (DRIE), and ion beam etching.
    • Dry etching provides higher resolution, better directionality, and cleaner etch profiles compared to wet etching but requires more complex equipment.

After that the unexposed portions of the photoresist can be removed by a chemical leaving the patterned SiO2.


Image: Etching

6. Gate Oxide Growth

Gate oxide growth in MOSFET fabrication is a critical process step that involves forming a thin insulating layer of silicon dioxide (SiO2) on the silicon wafer surface where the transistor's gate will be located

  • The gate oxide serves as the dielectric layer between the gate electrode and the silicon channel.
  • It allows the gate voltage to induce an inversion layer (channel) in the substrate to conduct current between source and drain.
  • The gate oxide layer is formed by thermal oxidation of the silicon surface.

Image: Gate oxide growth

7. Polysilicon deposition

  • Polysilicon deposition in fabrication is a critical process that creates the gate electrode structure, which controls the device channel.
  • This process involves depositing a thin layer of polycrystalline silicon (polysilicon) on the wafer and then precisely shaping it to form the transistor gate.
  • Material: Polysilicon is a polycrystalline form of silicon made up of small silicon crystals (grains). It is used because it can be heavily doped to become conductive and withstand high processing temperatures.
  • Deposition Method: The most common technique for depositing polysilicon is Low-Pressure Chemical Vapor Deposition (LPCVD).
  • In this process, silane gas (SiH4) is introduced into a high-temperature deposition chamber (typically around 600–650°C).
  • Chemical Reaction: The silane gas decomposes on the heated wafer surfaces, depositing a uniform layer of polysilicon:

SiH4→Si+2H2

  • Thickness Control: The thickness of the polysilicon layer is controlled by deposition time and temperature, enabling layers typically in the range of 100 to 300 nm.

Image: Polysilicon Deposition

After deposition, the polysilicon layer is patterned and etched to form gate of the MOSFET.


Image: Polysilicon Etching

The thin gate oxide not covered by polysilicon is also etched away, which exposes the silicon surface on which the source and drain junctions are to be formed.


Image: Gate oxide Etching

8. Source and Drain Doping

  • Source and Drain doping in MOSFET fabrication is a vital step that creates the highly doped regions adjacent to the gate, which form the transistor's source and drain terminals.
  • These regions enable current flow when the device is switched on and are critical to the MOSFET's electrical characteristics.

Purpose:

  • The source and drain regions are heavily doped (n+ for NMOS, p+ for PMOS) to create low-resistance contacts.
  • These regions allow electrons (or holes) to flow efficiently into and out of the transistor channel, which forms beneath the gate oxide.

Doping Technique:

  • Ion Implantation is the most common method used for doping the source and drain regions.
  • In ion implantation, ions of the dopant element (e.g., phosphorus or arsenic for n-type, boron for p-type) are accelerated to high energies and directed at the wafer.
  • The ions penetrate the silicon surface and embed at a specific depth determined by their energy.

Self-Aligned Process:

  • The gate electrode, made of polysilicon, acts as a mask during ion implantation.
  • This ensures that the source and drain regions are precisely aligned to the gate edges, minimizing overlap capacitance and improving device performance.
  • The gate and a thin oxide layer protect the channel area from doping.

Doping Concentration and Profiles:

  • The doping concentration in source and drain regions is very high (typically 10^19 to 10^21 atoms/cm³) to ensure good conductivity.
  • The depth and concentration profile of the dopants are carefully controlled to optimize device characteristics like drive current, leakage, junction capacitance, and short-channel effects.

Image: Source and Drain Doping

9. Interlayer Oxide Deposition and Contact Opening in MOSFET Fabrication

Interlayer Oxide Deposition:

  • After the source, drain, and gate formation, the entire wafer surface is covered with an insulating layer called the interlayer dielectric (ILD), usually made of silicon dioxide (SiO2) or other dielectric materials.
  • This oxide layer electrically isolates the different device regions and metal interconnect layers to prevent unwanted current leakage or short circuits.
  • The deposition method often used is Chemical Vapor Deposition (CVD), which produces a uniform and conformal insulating film over the wafer surface.
  • Every time multiple dielectric layers with different properties are stacked or planarized by Chemical Mechanical Polishing (CMP) to achieve a smooth surface for metallization.

Image: interlayer oxide deposition

Contact Opening Process:

  • To electrically connect the MOSFET source, drain, and gate terminals with metal interconnects, openings called contact holes or vias must be created in the interlayer oxide.
  • Photolithography is used to coat the wafer with a photoresist and pattern it to expose areas where contacts are needed.
  • The exposed oxide is selectively removed by an etching process, typically a plasma-based dry etch such as Reactive Ion Etching (RIE), which provides anisotropic etching for precise, vertical contact holes.
  • The etching stops carefully at the underlying silicon or polysilicon regions without damaging them.
  • These contact holes expose the silicon surface or doped regions where metal will later be deposited to form electrical contacts.
  • Oxide spacers may be formed along hole sidewalls to improve isolation and prevent shorting.

Image: Contact opening

10. Metallization

  • Metallization in MOSFET fabrication is the process of depositing and patterning metal layers on the wafer to create electrical interconnections between the various components of the integrated circuit, such as the source, drain, gate, and other devices.
  • This step is crucial for enabling the flow of electrical signals within the chip and connecting the transistor terminals to external circuitry.

Purpose:

  • To form conductive pathways (interconnects) between the elements of the MOSFET and other devices.
  • To create contacts and bonding pads for external connections.
  • To ensure low-resistance electrical connections critical for high-speed device operation.

Common Metals Used:

  • Aluminium is the most traditional and commonly used metal due to its good conductivity and ease of deposition.
  • Copper is increasingly used in advanced technology nodes because of its lower resistivity and better electromigration resistance.
  • Other metals like titanium, tungsten, titanium nitride, and tantalum may be used for barrier layers or specialized contacts.

Metal Deposition Techniques:

  • Physical Vapor Deposition (PVD): Includes sputtering and evaporation.
    • Sputtering involves bombarding a metal target with ions (usually argon ions), causing metal atoms to eject and deposit onto the wafer surface. It provides uniform coverage and is widely used in modern fabs.
    • Evaporation uses thermal energy or electron beams to vaporize metal, which then condenses on the wafer.
  • Chemical Vapor Deposition (CVD): Sometimes used for metal layers or barrier/liner deposits.

Patterning:

  • After metal deposition, photoresist is applied and patterned using photolithography to define the metal interconnect layout.
  • The unwanted metal is removed by etching (wet chemical or dry plasma etching), leaving behind the desired metal lines and contacts.
  • Alternatively, lift-off processes can be used where metal is deposited over a patterned photoresist and then the resist is removed, lifting off unwanted metal.

Planarization:

  • Chemical Mechanical Polishing (CMP) is often used to planarize the metal and dielectric surfaces, providing a flat surface for subsequent layers.
  • This is critical for multilayer metallization in complex integrated circuits.

Image: Metal Deposition

Finally, the metal layer is patterned and etched, completing the interconnection of the MOS transistors on the surface.


Image: Metal Etching

❓ Check Your Understanding

1. What material is most commonly used for making wafers in semiconductor fabrication?

  • Polysilicon
  • Gallium Arsenide
  • Single-crystal silicon
  • Silicon dioxide

Answer: Single-crystal silicon

Single-crystal silicon is the most common material for semiconductor wafers due to its excellent semiconductor properties and the ability to grow large, high-purity crystals using the Czochralski process.

2. Which process is used to grow single-crystal silicon ingots for wafers?

  • Photolithography
  • Czochralski process
  • Ion implantation
  • Chemical Vapor Deposition

Answer: Czochralski process

The Czochralski process is the primary method for growing large, high-purity single-crystal silicon ingots used in semiconductor manufacturing.

3. Why is wafer surface polishing important before fabrication?

  • To increase its electrical conductivity
  • To add dopant atoms to the surface
  • To create circuit patterns on the wafer
  • To remove surface defects and achieve flatness

Answer: To remove surface defects and achieve flatness

Wafer polishing creates a mirror-smooth surface that is essential for precise photolithography and to ensure uniform device characteristics across the wafer.

4. What is the function of photolithography in semiconductor fabrication?

  • To grow a layer of silicon dioxide
  • To embed impurity atoms into the silicon lattice
  • To transfer patterns from a photomask onto the wafer
  • To deposit a thin film of metal on the wafer

Answer: To transfer patterns from a photomask onto the wafer

Photolithography uses light-sensitive photoresist and photomasks to transfer circuit patterns onto the wafer surface, enabling selective processing of different areas.

5. In oxidation, what layer is formed on silicon, and what is its primary role?

  • Silicon nitride; it acts as a hard mask
  • Polysilicon; it acts as a conductor
  • Photoresist; it is used for patterning
  • Silicon dioxide (SiO₂); it acts as an insulator

Answer: Silicon dioxide (SiO₂); it acts as an insulator

Oxidation forms a silicon dioxide layer that serves as an excellent electrical insulator, used for gate oxides, field oxides, and masking layers in semiconductor fabrication.

6. Which statement correctly differentiates between wet and dry etching?

  • Wet etching is anisotropic and uses plasma; dry etching is isotropic and uses liquid chemicals
  • Wet etching is for metals and dry etching is for insulators
  • Wet etching uses liquid chemicals and is isotropic; dry etching uses plasma and can be anisotropic
  • Wet etching and dry etching are two terms for the same process

Answer: Wet etching uses liquid chemicals and is isotropic; dry etching uses plasma and can be anisotropic

Wet etching uses chemical solutions and etches equally in all directions (isotropic), while dry etching uses plasma and can achieve directional etching (anisotropic) for more precise pattern transfer.

7. Which doping method uses high-energy ion beams to precisely insert impurity atoms into silicon?

  • Diffusion
  • Sputtering
  • Oxidation
  • Ion implantation

Answer: Ion implantation

Ion implantation accelerates dopant ions to high energies and implants them precisely into the silicon substrate, allowing for accurate control of doping concentration and depth.

8. In CMOS technology, what does the "C" for "Complementary" signify?

  • The circuit uses both analog and digital components
  • The circuit uses both NMOS and PMOS transistors
  • The transistors are arranged in a complementary, circular pattern
  • The process uses both silicon and germanium

Answer: The circuit uses both NMOS and PMOS transistors

CMOS (Complementary Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor) technology uses complementary pairs of NMOS and PMOS transistors, which enables low static power consumption in digital circuits.

9. What is the primary reason for forming n-wells and p-wells in CMOS fabrication?

  • To serve as the gate electrode for the transistors
  • To act as the main conductive interconnects
  • To provide isolated regions for PMOS and NMOS transistors
  • To protect the wafer from atmospheric contaminants

Answer: To provide isolated regions for PMOS and NMOS transistors

N-wells and p-wells create electrically isolated regions in the substrate that allow both NMOS and PMOS transistors to be fabricated on the same chip in CMOS technology.

10. What is the fundamental purpose of the gate oxide in a MOSFET?

  • To connect the gate directly to the channel for current flow
  • To act as a dopant source for the source/drain regions
  • To insulate the gate from the channel while enabling electric field control
  • To serve as the main structural support for the transistor

Answer: To insulate the gate from the channel while enabling electric field control

The gate oxide electrically insulates the gate electrode from the channel while allowing the gate voltage to create an electric field that controls current flow in the channel.

11. What material has traditionally been used for the gate electrode in a standard CMOS process?

  • Single-crystal silicon
  • Aluminium
  • Doped polysilicon
  • Silicon nitride

Answer: Doped polysilicon

Doped polysilicon has been the traditional material for gate electrodes because it can withstand high processing temperatures and can be heavily doped to become conductive.

12. Why is the formation of the source and drain in a modern MOSFET considered a "self-aligned" process?

  • The dopant atoms naturally align themselves with the crystal lattice
  • A highly precise, secondary alignment mask is used
  • The process automatically aligns to the edge of the wafer
  • The gate itself acts as a mask for the doping process

Answer: The gate itself acts as a mask for the doping process

In the self-aligned process, the gate electrode serves as a mask during ion implantation, ensuring that the source and drain regions are automatically aligned to the gate edges without requiring separate alignment steps.

📝 Key Takeaways

Wafer Preparation

Wafer Preparation is the foundation of semiconductor fabrication; it involves making and thoroughly cleaning a single-crystal silicon wafer to ensure a defect-free, contamination-free starting surface.

Oxidation

Oxidation grows a critical insulating SiO₂ layer that serves as gate oxide and/or field oxide.

Photolithography

Photolithography uses photoresist and photomasks to create precise patterns, enabling selective doping, oxide growth, and etching steps.

Etching

Etching (wet and dry) removes material according to patterned masks, forming device features and contact holes.

Doping

Doping adds specific atoms (n-type/p-type) via ion implantation to create source, drain, and wells, enabling both NMOS and PMOS transistors.

Interlayer Dielectric

Interlayer Dielectric Deposition insulates transistors before metal wiring; contact openings connect device areas to metal layers.

Metallization

Metallization deposits and patterns metals (usually Al or Cu) to wire up the source, drain, gate, and other device connections.

Testing and Dicing

Testing and dicing finalize the process, with chips cut from wafers and tested before packaging.
