Understanding fundamental principles of electronics and electronic components

🧠 Concept Overview

1. Introduction to electronics

What is Electronics?

  • Electronics is the science that deals with the development and application of devices and systems involving the flow of charge carriers, particularly in semiconductors
  • It is the study of how charge flows through various materials and devices like semiconductors, conductors, transistors, resistors, capacitors, inductors
  • All electronic applications involve the transmission of power

Atomic Structure

  • Matter is composed of molecules, which are made up of atoms
  • According to Bohr's theory, an atom consists of a positively charged nucleus and negatively charged electrons that revolve around the nucleus

Electron Behaviour

  • Excitation: This occurs when an electron absorbs energy and jumps from a lower energy orbit to a higher energy orbit
  • Ionization: If an electron gains enough energy to be completely removed from the atom, the atom becomes ionized
  • Valence Electrons: These are the electrons located in the outermost orbit of an atom

Electrical quantities

Charge (Q)

The physical property of matter that causes it to experience a force when placed in an electromagnetic field is called charge. Represented by q or Q . The SI unit of electrical charge is coulomb.

Current

The rate of flow of charge is called current. Units are amperes (A).

I = Q / T

  • I represent electric current
  • Q represents electric charge
  • T represents time, measured in seconds (s)

Voltage(V)

Electrical voltage is defined as electric potential difference between two points of an electric field.

SI unit is volts(v).

V = φ2 - φ1 , V = E / Q

V is the voltage between point 2 and 1 in volts (V).

φ1 and φ2 are the electric potentials

Electrical Resistance (R)

The force that opposes the flow of electric current is called resistance. SI units are ohms (Ω).

R = V / I

R stands for Resistance, V stands for Voltage, I stands for Current

Ohm's law

At constant temperature, the current(I) flowing through the material is proportional to the voltage(V) applied across it. I α V.

V = I R

Power(P)

It is defined as the rate energy is transformed or transferred over time. We measure power in units of joules/second, also known as watts.

P = I V

Key Formulas

Ohm's Law: V = I × R

Power: P = I × V

Current: I = Q / T

Resistance: R = V / I

2. Materials

Energy Band Formation

  • Close Packing: In solids, atoms are packed tightly together
  • Orbital Overlap: This proximity causes the electron orbitals of adjacent atoms to overlap
  • Band Creation: Instead of discrete energy levels found in single atoms, this overlap creates continuous bands of closely packed energy levels

Valence Band

  • It is the highest energy band that is completely filled with electrons at absolute zero temperature
  • This band is formed from the energy levels of the valence electrons
  • Electrons in the valence band are typically bound to their atoms and do not contribute to electrical conduction

Conduction Band

  • It is the lowest energy band that is mostly empty of electrons
  • This band lies at a higher energy level, just above the valence band
  • When electrons gain enough energy, they can jump from the valence band into the conduction band
  • Electrons in the conduction band are called free electrons and are responsible for conducting electricity

Forbidden-gap

The gap between the conduction and valence band is called Forbidden gap. The materials are classified based on this forbidden gap. If the electron needs to be made free from the nucleus in the energy band, the equal energy of forbidden gap shall be applied to it.


Image: Energy Band Diagram

Based on the size of the forbidden gap between conduction and valence band the materials are classified as follows:

1. Insulators

The materials that do not allow electric current pass through them are called insulators.

Examples: SiO2, SOI, wood, rubber etc.


Image: Energy Band gap in insulators

Characteristics of insulator
  • The Forbidden energy gap is very large
  • Valance band electrons are bound tightly to atoms
  • The value of forbidden energy gap for an insulator will be of 10eV

2. Semiconductors

The materials in which the forbidden energy gap is small and the conduction takes place if some external energy is applied are called semiconductors.

Examples: Silicon (Si), Germanium (Ge)


Image: Energy Band gap in Semiconductors

Characteristics of semiconductors
  • The Forbidden energy gap is very small
  • The forbidden gap for Si is 1.1eV, Ge is 0.7eV
  • A Semiconductor actually is neither an insulator, nor a good conductor
  • As the temperature increases, the conductivity of a semiconductor increases

3. Conductors

The materials that allow electric current pass through them are called conductors. The forbidden gap disappears between both the valence and conduction band.

Examples: Copper, Aluminium


Image: Energy Band gap in Conductors

Characteristics of conductor
  • The valance band and the conduction band gets overlapped
  • The free electrons available for conduction are many
  • If there's a slight increase in voltage, it increases the conduction

Material Classification Summary

Conductors: Overlapping bands, many free electrons (e.g., Copper, Aluminum)

Semiconductors: Small band gap (1.1eV for Si, 0.7eV for Ge), controlled conductivity

Insulators: Large band gap (~10eV), no free electrons for conduction

3. Semiconductors

  • Semiconductors can be pure elements, such as silicon or germanium, or compounds such as gallium arsenide or cadmium selenide
  • Semiconductors have negative temperature co-efficient and the resistance in semiconductors increase with the decrease in temperature and vice versa
  • The Conducting properties of a Semiconductor changes when a suitable metallic impurity is added to it, which is a very important property

Common Materials

The two most widely used semiconductor materials are silicon (Si) and germanium (Ge). Silicon is the backbone of the electronics industry because it is abundant and easy to purify.

How Do Semiconductors Work?

  • Energy Input: When a semiconductor absorbs energy, such as from heat or light, it can excite electrons
  • Free Electrons: An excited electron gains enough energy to break free from its atom and move throughout the material. These mobile electrons are negative charge carriers
  • Hole Creation: When an electron leaves its position, it creates a vacancy called a hole
  • Holes as Charge Carriers: This hole acts as a positive charge carrier. A nearby electron can move into the hole, which effectively causes the hole to move in the opposite direction
  • Electrical Conduction: In a semiconductor, the flow of electric current is due to the combined movement of both free electrons (negative charges) and holes (positive charges)

4. Doping

Doping is the process of intentionally adding tiny amounts of other elements (called "impurities") to a pure semiconductor, such as silicon, to change its electrical properties and make it more suitable for use in electronic devices like diodes and transistors.

Image: Doping in Semi-conductors
Image: Classification of Semiconductor materials

Why is Doping Necessary?

  • Pure semiconductors (like pure silicon) do not conduct electricity well
  • By adding impurities, we can control how many free electrons or "holes" are available to carry current, making the material much more useful for electronics

Types of Doping

There are two primary types of doping:

  • n-type doping: Adds elements with more electrons than silicon (like phosphorus or arsenic). The extra electron is free to move, increasing electrical conductivity with negative charge carriers (electrons)
  • p-type doping: Adds elements with fewer electrons than silicon (like boron or gallium). This creates "holes," which are places where an electron is missing---these act as positive charge carriers

5. Diodes

  • A diode is a basic electronic component made by joining a p-type and n-type semiconductor, forming a p-n junction
  • It allows electric current to flow mainly in one direction and blocks current flow in the opposite direction
  • This property makes diodes useful as one-way valves for electrical current
Image: Exchange of Electrons and holes
Image: Formation of PN junction

Biasing of a PN diode

When a diode is connected in a circuit, it has two biased conditions with the given supply. They are Forward biased condition and Reverse biased condition.

a) Forward biased

  • When a diode is connected in a circuit, with its anode to the positive terminal and cathode to the negative terminal of the supply, then such a connection is said to be forward biased condition
  • When anode and cathode are connected to positive and negative terminal respectively, the holes in P-type and electrons in N-type tend to move across the junction, breaking the barrier
  • There will free flow of current with this where the barrier mostly gets eliminated

Image: Forward Biased PN junction

b) Reverse bias

  • When a diode is connected in a circuit, with its anode to the negative terminal and cathode to the positive terminal of the supply, then such a connection is said to be Reverse biased condition
  • Electrons are attracted towards the positive terminal and holes attracted towards the negative terminal
  • Hence both will be far from the potential barrier increasing the junction resistance and prevent the electrons to cross the junction
  • On increasing the reverse bias some minority carriers cross the junction there by constituting the reverse current
  • This reverse current is almost constant when the temperature is constant. But when this reverse voltage increases further, then a point called reverse breakdown
  • This occurs where an avalanche of current flows through the junction. This high reverse current damages the device
Image: Reverse Biased PN junction
Image: VI Characteristics of Diode

Diode Operation Summary

Forward Bias: Anode positive, Cathode negative → Current flows (0.7V drop for Si)

Reverse Bias: Anode negative, Cathode positive → No current flows (except small leakage)

Breakdown: High reverse voltage → Avalanche current → Device damage

❓ Check Your Understanding

1. What material conducts electricity very well?

  • Conductor
  • Insulator
  • Semiconductor
  • Resistor

Answer: Conductor

Explanation: Conductors like copper and aluminum have overlapping valence and conduction bands, allowing easy flow of electrons with minimal resistance.

2. Which material's conductivity can be controlled and is used in electronic devices?

  • Semiconductor
  • Conductor
  • Metal
  • Plastic

Answer: Semiconductor

Explanation: Semiconductors like silicon have controllable conductivity through doping, making them ideal for electronic devices like transistors and diodes.

3. What is doping in semiconductors?

  • Adding impurities to change conductivity
  • Removing all electrons from the material
  • Cooling the material to absolute zero
  • Polishing the surface of the material

Answer: Adding impurities to change conductivity

Explanation: Doping involves adding controlled amounts of impurities to semiconductors to create either n-type (extra electrons) or p-type (extra holes) materials.

4. What type of charge carriers are added in n-type semiconductors?

  • Electrons (negative charges)
  • Protons (positive charges)
  • Holes (positive charges)
  • Neutrons (neutral charges)

Answer: Electrons (negative charges)

Explanation: N-type semiconductors are created by adding elements with extra valence electrons (like phosphorus), providing free electrons as negative charge carriers.

5. A diode allows current to flow in which direction?

  • One direction only
  • Both directions
  • No direction
  • Only in a circular path

Answer: One direction only

Explanation: Diodes act as one-way valves for electrical current, allowing flow only when forward biased (anode positive, cathode negative).

6. What happens when a diode is forward biased?

  • Current flows
  • Current is blocked
  • The diode explodes
  • Resistance becomes infinite

Answer: Current flows

Explanation: In forward bias, the external voltage reduces the depletion region, allowing current to flow through the diode with a small voltage drop (0.7V for silicon).

7. According to Ohm's Law, Voltage (V) equals:

  • Current (I) × Resistance (R)
  • Current (I) ÷ Resistance (R)
  • Resistance (R) ÷ Current (I)
  • Current (I) + Resistance (R)

Answer: Current (I) × Resistance (R)

Explanation: Ohm's Law states that voltage equals current multiplied by resistance (V = I × R), defining the relationship between these three fundamental electrical quantities.

8. What is the typical forward voltage drop across a silicon diode?

  • 0.7V
  • 12V
  • 0V
  • 1.5V

Answer: 0.7V

Explanation: Silicon diodes typically have a forward voltage drop of about 0.7 volts when conducting current in forward bias condition.

📝 Key Takeaways

Electronics

Electronics is the study of controlling electric current using components like resistors, capacitors, diodes, and transistors.

Materials

Materials are classified as conductors (easy current flow), insulators (block current), and semiconductors (controlled conductivity).

Semiconductors

Semiconductors like silicon are fundamental to modern electronics due to their ability to control electric current.

Doping

Doping is adding impurities to semiconductors to create n-type (extra electrons) and p-type (extra holes) materials, essential for device functionality.

Diodes

Diodes are semiconductor devices that allow current to flow in one direction only, used in rectification and signal control.

Ohm's Law

Ohm's Law (V = I × R) relates voltage, current, and resistance, forming the basis for analysing circuits.

Practical Application

Understanding these basics enables designing, reading, and troubleshooting simple electronic circuits effectively.
