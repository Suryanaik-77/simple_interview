Transistors (MOSFET, BJT)

Understanding semiconductor devices for amplification and switching

🧠 Concept Overview

1. Classification of Transistor:


Image: Classification of Transistor

A transistor is a semiconductor device used to amplify or switch electrical signals and power.

  • It is a fundamental component in modern electronics, typically made of semiconductor material with at least three terminals.
  • By applying a voltage or current to one pair of terminals, the transistor controls the current flowing through another pair of terminals.
  • This controlled output power allows it to amplify signals. Transistors can act as switches, being either on or off, to regulate the flow of electronic signals in circuits.

Main Types of Transistors

1. Bipolar Junction Transistor (BJT):

A BJT is a three-terminal semiconductor device used primarily for amplification and switching of electrical signals.

It works by using both electrons and holes as charge carriers, hence the term bipolar.


Image: Bipolar Junction Transistor BJT

Construction

  • The BJT is made by stacking three layers of doped semiconductor materials.
  • These layers form two p-n junctions: one between the emitter and base, and one between the base and collector.
  • There are two types of BJTs depending on the doping arrangement:
    • NPN Transistor: Two n-type layers (emitter and collector) separated by a thin p-type layer (base).
    • PNP Transistor: Two p-type layers (emitter and collector) separated by a thin n-type layer (base).
  • The three terminals are:
    • Emitter (E): Heavily doped to inject charge carriers (electrons for NPN, holes for PNP).
    • Base (B): Very thin and lightly doped; controls the flow of carriers.
    • Collector (C): Moderately doped; collects carriers from the emitter through the base.

Image: Symbol of BJT

Working Principle

  • The BJT operates via current control where a small input current into the base controls a larger current flow between the emitter and collector.
  • For an NPN transistor:
    • The base-emitter junction is forward biased (allowing electrons to flow from emitter to base).
    • The base-collector junction is reverse biased (allowing those electrons to be swept into the collector).
    • A small base current controls a much larger collector current.
  • For a PNP transistor, the operation is similar but with reversed polarity (holes flow from emitter to collector).

Image: Cross Section view of BJT

Modes of Operation

  • Active Mode: Emitter-base junction is forward biased and base-collector junction is reverse biased. Used for amplification.
  • Cutoff Mode: Both junctions are reverse biased; transistor is off.
  • Saturation Mode: Both junctions are forward biased; transistor is fully on and acts as a closed switch.

Configurations:

There are three common ways to connect BJTs:

  • Common Emitter (CE): Most widely used; provides voltage and current gain with a 180° phase shift.
  • Common Base (CB): Provides voltage gain but no current gain; no phase shift.
  • Common Collector (CC): Provides current gain but no voltage gain; no phase shift.

Applications

  • Signal amplification in audio, radio, and other analog circuits.
  • Switching in digital circuits.
  • Oscillators and signal modulators.
  • Used in integrated circuits and discrete electronic devices.

2. Field Effect Transistor (FET)

A Field Effect Transistor (FET) is a three-terminal semiconductor device where the current conduction is controlled by an electric field.

FETs are voltage-controlled devices. They use either electrons or holes as the charge carriers (unipolar conduction).

Construction

  • A FET consists of a narrow semiconductor bar or channel made of either n-type or p-type material.
  • There are three terminals:
    • Source (S): The terminal through which carriers enter the channel.
    • Drain (D): The terminal through which carriers leave the channel.
    • Gate (G): The terminal that controls the conductivity of the channel by applying a voltage.
  • The gate is separated from the channel by a thin insulating layer (in MOSFETs) or a reverse-biased p-n junction (in JFETs).

Working Principle

  • The gate voltage creates an electric field that affects the width of the conductive channel between the source and drain.
  • In an n-channel FET, a positive voltage on the gate attracts electrons, enhancing the channel and increasing current flow between source and drain.
  • In a p-channel FET, a negative gate voltage attracts holes to enhance conduction.
  • The electric field modulates the channel's conductivity by depleting or enhancing charge carriers.
  • By controlling the gate voltage, the FET regulates the current flowing from source to drain, making it act like a voltage-controlled resistor or switch.

Types of FETs

  • JFET (Junction Field Effect Transistor): Gate forms a p-n junction with the channel. Operates mostly in depletion mode.

Image: Cross Section view of JFET

  • MOSFET: Gate separated from the channel by a thin oxide layer. Can operate in depletion or enhancement mode.

Image: Cross Section view of MOSFET

Characteristics

  • High input impedance (because the gate is insulated).
  • Low noise compared to BJTs.
  • Voltage-controlled: small changes in gate voltage can control large output currents.
  • Ideal for switching and amplification applications, especially in RF and digital circuits.

Operating Regions

  • Ohmic Region: FET acts like a voltage-controlled resistor; current increases linearly with drain-source voltage.
  • Saturation Region: FET acts as a constant current source; drain current is mostly independent of drain-source voltage.
  • Cutoff Region: Channel is "pinched off," and almost no current flows between source and drain.

Applications

  • Used in analog and digital circuits for amplification.
  • Used as electrically controlled switches.
  • Preferred in circuits requiring high input impedance and low noise.
  • Widely used in microprocessors and power electronics due to low power consumption.

Difference between MOSFET and BJT:


Image: Difference Between BJT and MOSFET

The move from Bipolar Junction Transistors (BJTs) to Field Effect Transistors (FETs) happened due to several advantages that FETs offer over BJTs in many applications.

Advantages of FETs over BJTs:

  • Voltage-Controlled Device: FETs are controlled by voltage applied to the gate, requiring almost no input current, leading to very low power consumption at the input. BJTs, on the other hand, are current-controlled devices requiring a continuous base current.
  • High Input Impedance: FETs have very high input impedance (several megaohms), which means they don't load the preceding circuit stage. BJTs have lower input impedance, which can affect signal levels.
  • Lower Noise: FETs generate less electrical noise, making them preferable for low-noise amplification and sensitive analog applications.
  • Better Thermal Stability: FETs have better thermal stability because they primarily rely on majority charge carriers and have less minority carrier leakage, which makes them more reliable under varying temperature conditions.
  • Less Sensitive to Radiation: FETs are less affected by radiation and other environmental factors, which enhances durability in harsh conditions.
  • Simpler Integration and Fabrication: FETs, especially MOSFETs, require less chip area in integrated circuits, allowing for higher packing density and more complex circuits on a single chip.
  • Lower Power Consumption: FETs consume less power during operation, which is crucial for battery-operated and portable devices.
  • Faster Switching in Many Digital Applications: Although older FETs switched slower, modern MOSFETs can switch very rapidly, making them crucial in high-speed digital electronics.

Limitations of BJTs that led to the switch:

  • BJT requires continuous base current for operation, causing higher power consumption.
  • BJTs have lower input impedance, potentially loading the driving circuit.
  • BJTs generate more noise compared to FETs.
  • Thermal runaway is a risk in BJTs due to lower thermal stability.
  • BJTs have more complex fabrication and larger chip area requirement.

❓ Check Your Understanding

1. What is the main function of a transistor?

  • To store energy in a magnetic field
  • To resist the flow of current
  • To amplify or switch electronic signals
  • To convert AC to DC

Answer: To amplify or switch electronic signals

Transistors are semiconductor devices primarily used for amplification of signals or as electronic switches in digital circuits.

2. What are the three terminals of a Bipolar Junction Transistor (BJT)?

  • Source, Gate, Drain
  • Anode, Cathode, Gate
  • Emitter, Base, Collector
  • Input, Output, Ground

Answer: Emitter, Base, Collector

BJTs have three terminals: Emitter (emits carriers), Base (controls current), and Collector (collects carriers).

3. What does the acronym FET stand for?

  • Forward Emission Transformer
  • Field-Effect Transistor
  • Fast Electron Transfer
  • Frequency-Enhancing Triode

Answer: Field-Effect Transistor

FET stands for Field-Effect Transistor, which operates using an electric field to control current flow.

4. How is the current between the collector and emitter in a BJT primarily controlled?

  • By the voltage at the collector
  • By the input current at the base
  • By the temperature of the transistor
  • By the voltage at the emitter

Answer: By the input current at the base

BJTs are current-controlled devices where a small base current controls a larger collector-emitter current.

5. How is the current between the source and drain in a FET primarily controlled?

  • By the voltage applied at the gate
  • By the current at the source
  • By the overall resistance of the circuit
  • By the input current at the drain

Answer: By the voltage applied at the gate

FETs are voltage-controlled devices where the gate voltage creates an electric field that controls source-drain current.

6. Which of the following are the two main types of BJTs?

  • JFET and MOSFET
  • N-Channel and P-Channel
  • Depletion and Enhancement
  • NPN and PNP

Answer: NPN and PNP

BJTs are classified as NPN (two n-type layers with p-type base) and PNP (two p-type layers with n-type base).

7. Which transistor type is known for having a significantly higher input impedance?

  • BJT
  • FET
  • Both have equally high impedance
  • Both have equally low impedance

Answer: FET

FETs have very high input impedance (megaohms range) due to insulated gate, while BJTs have much lower input impedance.

8. In terms of power consumption in a static state, which transistor type is generally more efficient?

  • BJT
  • FET
  • Both consume equal power
  • Power consumption is determined only by the circuit, not the transistor type

Answer: FET

FETs consume very little power in static state due to voltage control and high input impedance, making them more power-efficient.

9. What is the key difference between enhancement-mode and depletion-mode MOSFETs?

  • Enhancement-mode is normally ON; Depletion-mode is normally OFF
  • Enhancement-mode uses three terminals; Depletion-mode uses four
  • Enhancement-mode is normally OFF; Depletion-mode is normally ON
  • Enhancement-mode is for high-frequency; Depletion-mode is for high-power

Answer: Enhancement-mode is normally OFF; Depletion-mode is normally ON

Enhancement-mode MOSFETs require gate voltage to turn on, while depletion-mode MOSFETs conduct with zero gate voltage.

10. An FET is considered a "unipolar" device because it operates using:

  • Neither electrons nor holes
  • Both electrons and holes
  • Only one type of charge carrier (either electrons or holes)
  • A single pole magnetic field

Answer: Only one type of charge carrier (either electrons or holes)

FETs are unipolar devices because they use only one type of charge carrier (electrons in n-channel, holes in p-channel), unlike BJTs which use both.

📝 Key Takeaways

Transistor Fundamentals

Transistors are fundamental semiconductor devices used for amplification and switching in electronic circuits.

Two Main Types

BJT (current-controlled, bipolar) and FET (voltage-controlled, unipolar) are the two main transistor families.

BJT Types

BJTs come in NPN and PNP types with different charge carrier flow directions and terminal configurations.

FET Varieties

FETs include JFET and MOSFET types, operating in depletion and enhancement modes with insulated gates.

FET Advantages

FETs offer higher input impedance, lower power consumption, less noise, better thermal stability than BJTs.

Technology Evolution

The switch from BJT to FET technology enabled energy-efficient, miniaturized, high-speed electronic devices.
