📚 Knowledge Assessment Quiz

1. You run place_opt and observe that the WNS does not improve after 5 optimization iterations — it remains stuck at -0.95 ns. The congestion report shows 12% overflow on M3. What is the most likely reason the timing optimizer is failing to make progress?

  • The dont-use rules are too aggressive — the optimizer has no cells available to insert for timing improvement.
  • The opt.timing.effort setting is too low — changing it from medium to high will immediately resolve the WNS.
  • The 12% congestion overflow is preventing the optimizer from moving critical cells closer together — cells on the critical path cannot be legally placed nearer to each other because the routing layers around them are already saturated.
  • Concurrent Clock and Data (CCD) optimization must be enabled to break the timing convergence deadlock during place_opt.
Correct! When global routing congestion is high (above ~5%), the placement optimizer cannot freely move cells on the critical path because any repositioning would worsen congestion in an already-saturated region. The optimizer and congestion engines reach a deadlock — timing wants cells closer, but congestion demands spreading. The fix is to address congestion first: reduce max_density, increase cong_restruct_effort, or identify and spread the specific congested modules before running place_opt again.

2. After the placement run, analyze_design_violations -type max_trans shows 340 max-transition violations, all on the net scan_en which has 2,800 loads. Which action is the most appropriate first response?

  • Increase opt.common.max_fanout from 32 to 3000 to allow scan_en to drive all its loads without buffering.
  • Add scan_en to the dont-touch list so the optimizer stops trying to fix it and moves on to other violations.
  • Manually insert a balanced buffer tree on the scan_en net using synthesize_buf_tree or equivalent, then re-run place_opt so the optimizer works from a pre-buffered starting point with reasonable fanout per segment.
  • Remove scan_en from the SDC max-transition constraint so it is no longer reported as a violator.
Correct! A net with 2,800 loads is a high-fanout net that the automatic optimizer cannot buffer effectively in one pass — it needs a structured buffer tree inserted before place_opt so that each buffer in the tree drives a manageable load. Using synthesize_buf_tree or a manual buffer insertion command creates a balanced H-tree structure. After this pre-insertion, place_opt can optimize the individual segments of the tree within the fanout limit defined by opt.common.max_fanout.

3. A colleague argues that LVT cells should be enabled during place_opt for iguana_soc because the design has aggressive timing targets and SVT cells alone cannot close setup timing. What is the risk of enabling LVT cells at the placement stage rather than in post-route optimization?

  • The placement optimizer will use LVT cells aggressively across the entire design without any power constraint, potentially exceeding the leakage power budget by 2–5x before any power-timing tradeoff analysis is performed.
  • LVT cells have longer gate delays than SVT cells in ICC2's timing model, so enabling them during placement will worsen WNS rather than improve it.
  • LVT cells require a separate placement row type that is only defined in the post-route floorplan — enabling them during place_opt will cause check_legality failures.
  • There is no risk — ICC2 automatically limits LVT cell usage to the minimum needed to meet timing, regardless of whether LVT cells are excluded from the dont-use list.
Correct! When LVT cells are unrestricted during placement, the optimizer uses them freely on any timing-critical path — not just those that genuinely need the extra speed. This produces a design where LVT cells appear throughout the logic, often exceeding the chip-level leakage power specification by a large margin. The correct approach is to allow LVT cells only in post-route optimization, where power-aware timing analysis can ensure LVT usage is confined to paths where SVT genuinely cannot meet timing within the power budget.

4. You compare the report_congestion output from the tile (Guided Testcase) and iguana_soc (this Challenge). The iguana_soc shows 3.8% overflow on M4 while tile showed 0% overflow. What design characteristic of iguana_soc most likely explains this difference, and what placement setting should you adjust first?

  • iguana_soc uses more metal layers than tile, and the additional layers are causing routing conflicts on M4 that do not occur in single-layer designs.
  • The iguana_soc floorplan has a different aspect ratio that forces the global router to use M4 for both horizontal and vertical routing simultaneously, doubling the demand.
  • iguana_soc has a higher cell count and more complex connectivity than tile, creating larger routing demand per unit area. Reducing place.coarse.max_density from 0.7 to 0.65 and increasing cong_restruct_effort to high will give the placer more room to spread cells and reduce M4 overflow.
  • The congestion difference is entirely due to the IO buffer insertion — iguana_soc has more ports than tile, and the IO buffers near the boundary are consuming M4 routing resources.
Correct! A larger, more complex SoC like iguana_soc has higher routing demand per unit core area due to greater cell count and longer average net lengths. Reducing max_density from 0.7 to 0.65 provides more routing headroom per placement tile, and increasing cong_restruct_effort allows the coarse placer to spread logic clusters more aggressively away from M4 congestion hotspots. These two adjustments together are typically sufficient to bring M4 overflow below 1% for SoC-scale designs at this technology node.
