🤔 Before We Begin — Think About This

Your chip has gone through synthesis, placement, clock tree synthesis, routing, and post-route optimization. Each of these stages may have silently modified your netlist — renaming registers, merging flip-flops, restructuring logic cones, or inserting buffers in clock paths.

❓ How do you mathematically prove that the netlist you are about to tape out still behave exactly what your gate-level netlist from synthesis intended?

The answer is Logical Equivalence Checking (LEC) — and that's precisely what this document will teach you.

📖 Theory and Background

To understand where LEC is needed, you need to see the full picture of the VLSI design flow and where netlists are generated:

Stage What Happens Netlist Output LEC Check Needed?
Logic Synthesis RTL is compiled into standard cells from a technology library Gate-Level Netlist (post-synth) ✅ RTL-to-Gate LEC
Placement & Routing (PnR) Cells are placed on die, wires routed; clock tree inserted; optimizations done PnR Netlist (post-PnR) ✅ Gate-to-Gate LEC — We are here
⚠️ Why PnR can change your netlist: During PnR, the EDA tool performs placement, clock tree synthesis (CTS), routing, and several optimization passes. These include buffer insertion, gate resizing, register retiming, cell swapping, and post-route ECO (Engineering Change Order) fixes. Each of these can alter logic structure. Without G2G LEC, you cannot be sure the PnR output is still functionally identical to the synthesized netlist.

🔍 What is Gate-to-Gate (G2G) LEC?

In this document, you are learning about a specific type of LEC called Gate-to-Gate (G2G). Here, both the reference and implementation designs are gate-level netlists — not RTL.


  • Reference Design (Golden): The gate-level netlist output from synthesis (post-synthesis, pre-PnR). This is the design that was verified to be correct after RTL-to-Gate LEC.
  • Implementation Design (Revised): The gate-level netlist output after Place and Route (PnR). This is what you are validating.

Your job is to prove that the PnR netlist is still functionally equivalent to the synthesis netlist, even after all the physical implementation transformations that happened in between.

Test your understanding of G2G LEC concepts covered in this document. Click on your answer for each question.

1. In a Gate-to-Gate (G2G) LEC run, what serves as the reference design (the Golden design)?

  • RTL (Verilog source code)
  • The post-synthesis gate-level netlist
  • The post-PnR gate-level netlist
  • The final GDSII layout
Correct! In G2G LEC, the reference design is the post-synthesis netlist. It acts as the "Golden" design because it has already been verified to match the original intent during the earlier RTL-to-Gate LEC run.

2. In the same Gate-to-Gate (G2G) LEC run, what serves as the implementation design (the Revised design)?

  • RTL (Verilog source code)
  • The post-synthesis gate-level netlist
  • The post-PnR gate-level netlist
  • The final GDSII layout
Correct! The implementation design is the post-PnR netlist. The ultimate goal of G2G LEC is to prove that the physical implementation tool didn't break any of the logic established during synthesis.

3. Why might the Placement & Routing (PnR) EDA tool silently modify the netlist?

  • To compile the design from RTL code into technology standard cells.
  • To verify that the Golden and Revised designs are mathematically equivalent.
  • To optimize the physical layout through buffer insertion, clock tree synthesis (CTS), gate resizing, and routing detours.
  • To upgrade the technology library database to a newer standard.
Correct! During the PnR phase, the EDA tool performs multiple optimization passes (like placement, CTS, routing, and post-route ECO fixes) that can alter the logic structure, which is why a subsequent G2G LEC check is absolutely required.
