📝 Knowledge Assessment

1. During global placement, cell overlaps are permitted. Why does the tool allow this, and when are overlaps resolved?

  • Overlaps are not permitted — global placement produces a legal solution with no overlaps from the start
  • Overlaps are permitted only for macro blocks; standard cells are always placed legally
  • Global placement focuses on minimizing total wirelength and achieving good density distribution across the chip. Enforcing legality (no overlaps) simultaneously would make the optimization problem computationally intractable. Overlaps are resolved in the detailed placement phase, which legalizes every cell to its final site without overlap
  • Overlaps are permitted as a tool preference setting — they can be disabled, but runtime increases significantly
Correct! Global placement solves a massive multi-objective optimization problem: positioning millions of cells to minimize wirelength, balance density, and prepare for timing optimization. Adding a hard "no-overlap" constraint at this stage would transform it into an NP-hard combinatorial problem that no tool could solve in reasonable time. Instead, global placement treats cells as continuous objects that can overlap, finds an approximately good solution, and then hands off to detailed placement which legalizes each cell to its nearest available site row while preserving the coarse solution quality.

2. Dynamic power in CMOS is expressed as P = α × C × V² × f. What does the factor α (alpha) represent?

  • The process technology parameter that accounts for transistor leakage at a given voltage
  • The ratio of clock frequency to the maximum achievable frequency for the design
  • The switching activity factor — the average probability that a node transitions from 0→1 (or 1→0) per clock cycle. A value of 0.1 means on average the node switches 10% of cycles
  • The fraction of cells that are active (not clock-gated) at any given time
Correct! The switching activity factor (α) captures how often a net actually switches per clock cycle. A combinational output tied to a constant signal has α ≈ 0; a clock net has α = 1 (toggles every cycle). Dynamic power is only consumed when a node switches — charging the net's capacitance to VDD or discharging it to GND. Power optimization strategies like clock gating, operand isolation, and reduced logic depth all work by reducing the effective α of the busiest nodes in the design.

3. After placement, a GRC (Global Routing Congestion) analysis reports overflow in a region. What does "overflow" mean in this context?

  • The number of placed cells in the region exceeds the maximum placement density setting
  • More wires are routed through the region than the DRC minimum spacing allows, causing shorts
  • The routing demand (number of nets that need to pass through a routing cell) exceeds the available routing supply (number of routing tracks on available metal layers). Every unit of overflow represents a net that cannot be routed without a detour
  • The power grid stripes in the region have consumed too many routing tracks, leaving insufficient space for signal routing
Correct! Global routing divides the chip into a grid of routing cells (GRCs). For each GRC, the tool computes the routing demand (how many nets need to pass through) and the supply (how many tracks are available on each metal layer). When demand exceeds supply, the overflow count increases. High overflow indicates the design will be difficult or impossible to route without placement adjustments — common fixes include increasing cell spacing in the congested area, moving macros to open routing channels, or adding routing blockages to redirect traffic.

4. In a standard power grid hierarchy, what is the role of "follow-pin" straps (also called M1 rails)?

  • Follow-pin straps are the widest metal layer in the power grid, carrying the highest current from the package bumps to the power ring
  • Follow-pin straps are horizontal M4–M6 power stripes that run above the standard cell rows and supply current to vertical stripes
  • Follow-pin straps are the M1 VDD and VSS rails that run horizontally within every standard cell row. Every standard cell connects its power supply directly to these rails — they are the last segment of the power delivery hierarchy from ring → stripes → M1 rails → cell supply pins
  • Follow-pin straps are via stacks that connect the upper power mesh to the M1 rails at the boundary of each standard cell row
Correct! The power delivery hierarchy flows: package pads → chip power ring → mid-metal power stripes → M1 follow-pin rails → cell supply pins. Each standard cell has VDD and VSS pins that connect directly to the M1 rails running through the cell row. These M1 rails are called "follow-pin" because they follow the row boundaries across the entire width of the chip. The upper-metal stripes periodically connect down to these M1 rails via stacked vias to ensure low-resistance supply everywhere.
