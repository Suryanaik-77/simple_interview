📚 Knowledge Assessment Quiz

1. What is the primary goal of Clock Tree Synthesis (CTS) in the PnR flow?

  • To minimize the total wire length of all signal nets in the design
  • To build a balanced clock distribution network that delivers the clock signal to all flip-flops with controlled skew and minimized insertion delay
  • To perform hold violation fixing by inserting delay cells on all data paths
  • To route all clock nets on the top metal layers to avoid congestion with signal nets
Correct! CTS builds a physical clock distribution tree — a hierarchy of clock buffers and wires — that drives the clock signal from the source to every sequential element (flip-flop, latch, ICG). The two key quality metrics are skew (difference in arrival time between the earliest and latest clock sinks) and insertion delay (total latency from clock source to sink). Minimizing both enables setup and hold timing closure.

2. Why are clock nets routed with Non-Default Rules (NDR) — typically double width and double spacing — instead of the minimum design rules used for signal nets?

  • NDR rules reduce clock wire resistance, which lowers insertion delay and allows the clock to reach more sinks with fewer buffers
  • The router requires NDR rules to distinguish clock nets from signal nets during global routing
  • Wider wires reduce resistance and coupling capacitance variation, giving more consistent RC delay across the clock tree. Wider spacing reduces crosstalk-induced noise from adjacent signal nets, which can cause glitches on the clock waveform and introduce jitter
  • NDR rules prevent the clock net from being used as a shielding reference for signal nets during detailed routing
Correct! Clock nets are routed with double-width, double-spacing NDR rules to reduce resistance (improving signal integrity), reduce coupling capacitance variation (improving skew predictability), and minimize crosstalk from adjacent switching signal nets. Crosstalk on a clock net is especially harmful because it causes jitter — cycle-to-cycle variation in clock arrival time — which consumes timing budget that cannot be recovered by any optimization.

3. What is the difference between ideal clocks (used during placement) and propagated clocks (used after CTS)?

  • Ideal clocks use the actual routed clock network for timing; propagated clocks use the SDC clock period only
  • There is no difference — ICC2 always uses propagated clocks regardless of the flow stage
  • Ideal clocks assume zero insertion delay and zero skew — the clock arrives at every flip-flop simultaneously at time zero. Propagated clocks use the actual clock tree network to compute the real arrival time at each sink, including insertion delay and skew
  • Ideal clocks are used only for hold analysis; propagated clocks are used only for setup analysis
Correct! During placement, ICC2 uses ideal clocks — the clock is treated as arriving at every flip-flop at the same instant with zero latency. This simplifies placement optimization because skew is unknown before the clock tree exists. After CTS, ICC2 switches to propagated clocks, where the real clock tree network (buffers, wires) is used to compute the actual arrival time at each sink. Post-CTS setup WNS may appear worse than post-placement WNS because the clock insertion delay is now included in the timing calculation.

4. Why is hold violation fixing performed during the final_opto phase of CTS rather than during placement?

  • The placement tool does not have access to hold timing constraints — they are only available after the clock tree is built
  • Hold violations only occur on paths between different clock domains, which cannot be identified until the clock tree topology is known
  • Hold violations are caused by clock skew between the launch and capture flip-flops. Under ideal clocks during placement, skew is zero, so hold analysis produces misleading results. Only after CTS — when real skew exists — can hold violations be accurately identified and fixed with delay cells
  • Hold fixing during placement would require inserting delay cells that increase wire length, which conflicts with the placement congestion reduction goals
Correct! Hold violations are fundamentally a skew problem. A hold violation occurs when the launch clock arrives earlier than the capture clock by more than the data path delay — the data changes before the capture flip-flop has safely stored the previous value. Under ideal clocks (zero skew), this condition cannot occur on same-clock-domain paths. Only after CTS introduces real clock skew can the tool identify which paths are genuinely at risk and insert the minimum necessary hold buffers during final_opto.
