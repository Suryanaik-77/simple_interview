📖 What is Floorplanning?

Floorplanning transforms a logically initialized design into a physical canvas. It answers the question: "Where does everything go?"

  • Define the die boundary and core area — the legal region where standard cells and macros can be placed.
  • Set core utilization — the percentage of core area occupied by cells (typically 65–75%).
  • Place hard macros (SRAMs, memory blocks) at specific locations using a DEF file or manually.
  • Assign I/O ports to the block boundary so signals can connect to the outside world.
  • Insert physical-only cells: boundary cells (endcaps) protect row ends; tap cells prevent latch-up.
  • Build the power distribution network (PDN): M1 rails → fine mesh (M4/M5) → mid-level stripes (M6/M7) → top-level trunks (M8/M9).

🔢 Steps in the Floorplan Stage

  1. Open library and block — load the NDM and open the init_design checkpoint.
  2. Initialize floorplan — define die size, core utilization, and core offsets.
  3. Port placement — create a pin guide boundary, then place all ports on that boundary.
  4. Set routing layer directions — assign horizontal/vertical routing to each metal layer.
  5. Import DEF — read macro placement from floorplan.def.
  6. Insert boundary cells — protect row ends using library endcap cells.
  7. Create PG nets and ports — declare VDD/VSS nets and block-level power ports.
  8. Connect PG to cells — connect power rails to all standard cell and macro pins.
  9. Set PG app options — configure tool settings to handle SRAM macro blockages correctly.
  10. Build power grid — source power_plan.tcl to create M1 rails through M9 stripes and all inter-layer vias.
  11. Insert tap cells — place well-tap cells in a stagger pattern for latch-up prevention.
  12. Save floorplan block — checkpoint as floorplan in the NDM.
  13. Run floorplan checks — generate five quality reports.

📝 Knowledge Assessment Quiz

Test your understanding of the floorplanning concepts covered above. Click on your answer.

1. You set -core_utilization 0.7 in initialize_floorplan. What does this mean?

  • 70% of the die area is reserved for I/O pads.
  • 70% of the core area will be occupied by standard cells and macros; 30% is left free for routing and optimization.
  • The tool will stop placing cells once 70 cells have been placed.
  • The clock tree will use 70% of the available routing resources.
Correct! Core utilization (70%) defines the ratio of cell area to core area. The remaining 30% provides routing channels, space for clock tree buffers, and margin for timing optimization during placement and routing.

2. Why must endcap (boundary) cells be inserted at the ends of standard cell rows?

  • They act as decoupling capacitors to reduce IR drop.
  • They fill unused space to meet metal density requirements.
  • They absorb the Well Proximity Effect (WPE) at row boundaries, preventing threshold voltage shifts in nearby functional cells.
  • They provide additional drive strength for output buffers.
Correct! Endcap cells (boundary cells) sit at the ends of every standard cell row. Their primary purpose is to absorb the Well Proximity Effect (WPE) — a manufacturing phenomenon where transistors near well edges have shifted Vth. Without endcaps, functional cells at row ends would have unpredictable timing.

3. What is the purpose of well-tap cells, and what is the maximum allowed spacing between them in a typical 28nm process?

  • They insert filler metal to satisfy density rules; maximum spacing is 100 µm.
  • They buffer high-fanout signals on the VDD rail; spacing is 10 µm.
  • They tie N-well to VDD and P-substrate to VSS to prevent latch-up; maximum spacing is typically ~30 µm.
  • They isolate clock domains from logic; spacing is determined by the clock frequency.
Correct! Well-tap cells tie the N-well to VDD and the P-substrate to VSS, reverse-biasing the base-emitter junctions of the parasitic BJTs formed in CMOS. This prevents latch-up. Foundry rules for 28nm typically require taps within 30 µm of any transistor.

4. In a multi-layer power grid, why are wider metal stripes (e.g. 3 µm on M8/M9) used on upper layers while thinner wires (e.g. 0.15 µm) are used on M1/M4/M5?

  • Upper metals are farther from transistors so they need wider lines to satisfy the minimum width rule on those layers.
  • Thin wires on M1 reduce capacitive loading on the power rail and improve switching speed.
  • Upper metals carry the total chip current and must meet electromigration limits; lower metals carry only local currents to individual cells and can be narrow.
  • It is a manufacturing convenience — wider lines are easier to etch on the upper metal stacks.
Correct! Electromigration (EM) limits current density (mA/µm²). Upper metal layers (M8, M9) carry the aggregate current of the entire chip, so they must be wide enough to keep current density within the EM limit. Lower layers (M1 rails, M4/M5 mesh) distribute current to individual cells and carry much smaller currents, so narrow widths are acceptable.
